# A Critical Assessment of the Revised Bloom's Taxonomy

## Abstract

Anderson and Krathwohl's (2001) *A Taxonomy for Learning, Teaching, and Assessing: A Revision of Bloom's Taxonomy of Educational Objectives* has achieved near-canonical status in educational practice, yet its empirical foundations remain surprisingly fragile. This paper examines six major criticisms of the revised taxonomy: (1) the weak empirical support for its hierarchical structure; (2) the unreliability of action verbs as cognitive proxies; (3) the non-independence of its two-dimensional framework; (4) the artificial nature of discrete cognitive categories; (5) the misleading higher-order/lower-order distinction; and (6) its disconnection from contemporary cognitive science. While acknowledging the taxonomy's practical utility as a heuristic for instructional design, this analysis concludes that the dogmatic adherence common in educational practice is scientifically unjustified.

**Keywords:** Bloom's Taxonomy, Anderson and Krathwohl, cognitive processes, educational objectives, learning assessment, instructional design

---

## 1. Introduction

Few frameworks in educational practice enjoy the ubiquity of Bloom's Taxonomy. Originally published in 1956 as *Taxonomy of Educational Objectives: The Classification of Educational Goals* (Bloom et al., 1956), the framework was substantially revised by Anderson and Krathwohl in 2001. The revised version reorganised the original six cognitive categories—Knowledge, Comprehension, Application, Analysis, Synthesis, and Evaluation—into verb forms (Remember, Understand, Apply, Analyze, Evaluate, Create) and added an orthogonal knowledge-type dimension comprising Factual, Conceptual, Procedural, and Metacognitive knowledge (Anderson et al., 2001; Krathwohl, 2002).

The taxonomy's influence on educational practice is difficult to overstate. It underpins curriculum design, assessment construction, and learning objective formulation across virtually all educational levels and disciplines (Crowe et al., 2008; Momsen et al., 2010). Teacher training programmes routinely present it as foundational knowledge, and accreditation bodies frequently require learning outcomes aligned to its categories (Newton et al., 2020).

Yet this widespread adoption stands in curious tension with the taxonomy's empirical foundations. As Wilson (2016) observes, "teachers-in-training are rarely made aware of any of the criticisms leveled against Bloom's original model." This paper provides a systematic examination of those criticisms, arguing that while the taxonomy retains value as a rough heuristic, its treatment as a validated model of cognition is scientifically unjustified.

## 2. The Hierarchical Structure: An Empirical Problem

### 2.1 Original Evidence Against the Hierarchy

The foundational claim of Bloom's Taxonomy—that cognitive processes are arranged in a cumulative hierarchy from simple to complex—was questioned almost immediately after the original publication. A cumulative hierarchy assumes that mastery of each level is prerequisite to the next; performance on Knowledge questions should predict Comprehension performance, but not performance at Application or beyond (Stoker & Kropp, 1971).

Empirical investigations consistently failed to support this structure. Madaus, Woods, and Nuttall (1973) found that a single underlying factor predicted performance across all six cognitive categories, contradicting the assumption of distinct, sequentially-ordered processes. Seddon (1978) and Hill and McGaw (1981) demonstrated that the Knowledge category appeared to operate within a different structure entirely from the remaining five categories. Perhaps most damaging, Kropp, Stoker, and Bashaw (1966) found that Synthesis and Evaluation were reversed in terms of actual cognitive complexity—a finding that would later prompt the revision's reordering of these categories.

Hill and McGaw (1981) further identified direct connections between non-consecutive categories, such as Comprehension and Analysis, undermining the strictly linear progression the taxonomy assumes. As Kreitzer and Madaus (1994) summarised in their comprehensive review, the cumulative hierarchy model simply lacked empirical support.

### 2.2 The Revision's Response

The 2001 revision acknowledged these problems but addressed them only partially. The reordering of Synthesis (now Create) and Evaluation reflected the Kropp et al. (1966) findings, and the revision adopted what Krathwohl (2002) termed a "relaxed hierarchy"—an acknowledgment that the strict cumulative model could not be maintained.

However, as Larsen et al. (2022) note, "even though the cumulative hierarchy model of the original taxonomy did not have a strong basis in evidence, the revised taxonomy holds steady in its theoretical interpretation that both the knowledge-type and cognitive-process dimensions are organized from simple to complex and from concrete to abstract." The revision, in other words, preserved the hierarchical framework in principle while quietly abandoning it in practice—an uncomfortable theoretical position.

### 2.3 Contemporary Evidence

Recent research continues to challenge hierarchical assumptions. Agarwal (2019) demonstrated through controlled studies that students exposed to higher-order practice questions performed better on higher-order assessments without necessarily having mastered lower-order skills first. This directly contradicts the notion that lower-level mastery is prerequisite to higher-level performance. Notably, students who received mixed-level practice performed best overall, suggesting that the hierarchical scaffolding approach encouraged by the taxonomy may actually be suboptimal.

## 3. The Action Verb Problem

### 3.1 Verbs as Cognitive Proxies

One of the most practically consequential features of Bloom's Taxonomy is its association of specific action verbs with cognitive levels. The revised taxonomy codified its categories as verbs precisely to "emphasize the action focus" (Krathwohl, 2002). In practice, educators are routinely provided with verb lists—"describe" for Understanding, "analyze" for Analysis, "evaluate" for Evaluation—and instructed to construct learning objectives using appropriate verbs for their target cognitive level.

This practice assumes that verbs can serve as reliable proxies for cognitive processes: that using the verb "judge" will automatically require students to engage in Evaluation-level cognition. The assumption has motivated attempts to automate the classification of assessment items using verb analysis alone (Omar et al., 2012).

### 3.2 Empirical Disconfirmation

Research has thoroughly undermined this assumption. Stanny (2016) analysed verb lists from 30 educational websites in the United States and found "very little agreement between the versions of Bloom's Taxonomy found." The degree of disagreement was striking: not a single verb was assigned to the same tier by all 30 lists. Three verbs—choose, relate, and select—appeared in all six tiers depending on which list was consulted.

Newton, Da Silva, and Peters (2020) replicated and extended this analysis in the United Kingdom, collecting 47 publicly available verb lists from 35 universities and textbooks. Their findings were equally discouraging:

> There was very little agreement between these lists, most of which were not supported by evidence explaining where the verbs came from... From the 47 lists, we first identified verbs which appeared in >50% (24+) of the lists. From that list we then identified verbs for which 50% of their appearances were in one specific tier.

The resulting "master list" was notably sparse, reflecting the fundamental unreliability of verb-to-tier mappings.

### 3.3 Assessment Item Analysis

Larsen et al. (2022) examined whether verbs in actual assessment items could predict cognitive processes. Using the Shannon evenness index—a measure of distribution uniformity—they found that commonly used prompt words (both question words and action verbs) were distributed across multiple cognitive categories without clear associations. Even verbs traditionally considered strong indicators of specific levels ("describe," "explain," "identify") showed high evenness indices, indicating they appeared across multiple cognitive categories in actual use.

The researchers concluded that "prompt words do not reliably predict cognitive processes; thus, instructors creating assessment items and researchers categorizing them should not simply connect the prompt words in an assessment item with associated verbs and cognitive processes of the revised Bloom's taxonomy."

This finding has significant practical implications: the ubiquitous verb lists that accompany presentations of Bloom's Taxonomy may be actively misleading educators about what their assessments actually require.

## 4. The Independence Assumption

### 4.1 The Two-Dimensional Framework

The revised taxonomy's most significant structural innovation was the addition of a second dimension: knowledge types (Factual, Conceptual, Procedural, Metacognitive) conceived as orthogonal to cognitive processes. This created a 6×4 matrix in which any combination was theoretically possible—one could remember procedural knowledge, analyze factual knowledge, or create metacognitive knowledge (Anderson et al., 2001).

This structure was motivated partly by empirical evidence that knowledge types constituted a dimension distinct from cognitive processes (Seddon, 1978; Hill & McGaw, 1981). However, the taxonomy presents these dimensions as independent, with student learning occurring "at the intersection" (Krathwohl, 2002).

### 4.2 Evidence of Non-Independence

Larsen et al. (2022) directly tested this independence assumption using contingency analysis on 940 biology assessment items. Fisher's exact test revealed a statistically significant relationship between knowledge types and cognitive processes (p < 0.0001), meaning the dimensions were related rather than independent.

Correspondence analysis and hierarchical clustering revealed three predominant clusters:
- Factual knowledge with Remember
- Conceptual knowledge with Understand, Analyze, Evaluate, and Create
- Procedural knowledge with Apply

These clusters suggest that the elegant matrix structure may be partially illusory: certain combinations dominate in practice while others rarely occur. Whether this reflects intrinsic features of the knowledge-cognition relationship or merely habitual patterns in assessment design remains unclear, but either interpretation undermines the independence assumption.

### 4.3 Implications for Practice

The non-independence finding has practical consequences. In biology education research and practice, the knowledge-type dimension is frequently omitted, with researchers and instructors using only the cognitive-process dimension (Allen & Tanner, 2002; Crowe et al., 2008; Momsen et al., 2010, 2013). If the dimensions are correlated rather than independent, this simplification may be defensible—but it also means the two-dimensional framework adds less value than its theoretical presentation suggests.

## 5. The Artificiality of Discrete Categories

### 5.1 Cognition as Interconnected

A fundamental criticism of any taxonomic approach to cognition is that mental processes do not operate in discrete, separable stages. As noted in contemporary discussions, "the distinction between categories can be seen as artificial, since cognitive tasks often involve multiple processes. Categorizing mental processes into distinct classifications may undermine the interconnected nature of cognition" (Wikipedia, 2025).

When a student "analyzes" a scientific problem, they simultaneously retrieve relevant information from memory (Remember), construct meaning from that information (Understand), apply known procedures (Apply), and often evaluate hypotheses and create new ones—all within what might be classified as a single Analysis task. The taxonomy imposes clean boundaries on fundamentally interrelated processes.

### 5.2 Distinguishing Understand from Analyze

The difficulty of distinguishing between categories was a persistent problem with the original taxonomy and remains so with the revision. Larsen et al. (2022) devoted considerable attention to articulating a workable distinction between Understand and Analyze:

> To articulate a clear distinction between the two cognitive processes, we draw inspirations from Biggs's SOLO... In Biggs's SOLO, students demonstrate the unistructural learning outcome when they are able make a single generalization between two ideas. This best corresponded to our articulation of *understand*, a cognitive process that emphasizes the construction of a single relationship. When an assessment item asks students not only to draw multiple connections (multistructure) but also to piece together these relationships in the context of a larger whole (relational), students are asked to *analyze*.

That researchers needed to import an entirely separate theoretical framework (Biggs & Collis, 1982) to distinguish between two core categories illustrates the taxonomy's conceptual imprecision.

### 5.3 Interrater Reliability

The categorisation challenges manifest in interrater reliability data. Larsen et al. (2022) reported initial Fleiss' kappa values of 0.68 for knowledge types and 0.70 for cognitive processes—values characterised as "substantial agreement" but far from the near-perfect reliability one would expect if category boundaries were clear and unambiguous. More problematic than the raw numbers is the fact that consensus was achieved only through extensive discussion and iterative refinement of coding schemes, suggesting that naive application of the taxonomy would yield considerably lower agreement.

## 6. The Higher-Order/Lower-Order Distortion

### 6.1 The Pyramid Problem

The taxonomy's traditional pyramid representation creates an implicit value hierarchy. Remember and Understand, positioned at the base, become "lower-order thinking skills," while Analyze, Evaluate, and Create occupy the apex as "higher-order thinking skills." This visual metaphor has profoundly influenced educational discourse.

As Kagan (2003) observes:

> Among many educators this hierarchical notion of thinking has led to an unquestioned acceptance of the idea that there are "high" and "low" levels of thinking: Knowledge and Comprehension are presumed to be less complex cognitively than Application, Analysis, Synthesis, and Evaluation. Thus Knowledge and Comprehension have come to be viewed as "lower-level thinking" compared to Application, Analysis, Synthesis, and Evaluation, which are accepted as "higher-level thinking."

### 6.2 Complexity Within Categories

The fundamental problem with this characterisation is that cognitive complexity does not reside in the type of thinking but in the level at which that thinking is engaged. Kagan (2003) illustrates this compellingly:

> If I ask you if you like chocolate ice cream (an evaluation level question), the answer simply pops to mind as immediately and effortlessly as if I ask you if you ate chocolate ice cream within the last hour—a recall level question. If I ask you to recall all the times in the last month you ate or saw ice cream, the answer demands a great deal of cognitive effort, just as if I asked you to evaluate all the pros and cons of eating ice cream.

Evaluation, recall, and any other cognitive process can be engaged at simple or complex levels. The type of cognitive process is independent of the depth or difficulty at which it operates.

### 6.3 Pedagogical Consequences

The higher-order/lower-order distinction has generated what Booker (2007) terms "misdirection" in American education: "Bloom's Taxonomy has been used to devalue basic skills education and has promoted 'higher order thinking' at its expense" (p. 248). The pressure to write more "higher-order" objectives, regardless of whether foundational knowledge is established, may actually undermine effective learning.

Research by Agarwal (2019) suggests a more nuanced picture: students who received practice with both lower-order and higher-order questions outperformed those who received only one type. The hierarchical framework, with its implicit devaluation of "lower" levels, obscures this important finding.

## 7. Disconnection from Cognitive Science

### 7.1 Historical Context

The original Bloom's Taxonomy was developed in 1956, predating virtually all of modern cognitive science. The revision in 2001 incorporated some updates, but the fundamental architecture—discrete, hierarchical processing categories—does not map well onto contemporary understanding of how the brain processes information.

As Marzano (2000) observed in developing his alternative taxonomy, "the very structure of the Taxonomy, moving from the simplest level of knowledge to the most difficult level of evaluation, is not supported by research." The brain does not contain separate modules for "remembering" versus "understanding" versus "analyzing"; these are abstractions imposed on what is actually a distributed, parallel, and highly interconnected system.

### 7.2 Memory and Learning

Contemporary cognitive science distinguishes between different memory systems (working memory, long-term memory, procedural memory) in ways that do not align with the taxonomy's categories. The taxonomy treats "Remember" as the simplest cognitive process, yet retrieval from long-term memory involves complex reconstructive processes that interact with comprehension and evaluation in ways the taxonomy cannot capture.

Similarly, research on expertise (Bransford et al., 1999; National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine, 2018) emphasises that expert knowledge is characterised not by discrete facts but by meaningful patterns and connections—a finding more consistent with the importance of conceptual integration than with a hierarchy privileging "higher" over "lower" processes.

### 7.3 The Metacognitive Category

The revised taxonomy's addition of metacognitive knowledge represents a genuine advance, reflecting increased understanding of metacognition's importance in learning (Dauer et al., 2013). However, Larsen et al. (2022) found that "we did not identify any assessment items in our data set using metacognitive knowledge"—a finding suggesting disconnection between the theoretical completeness of the framework and its practical utility.

## 8. Defence of the Taxonomy as Heuristic

### 8.1 Practical Value

Despite the foregoing criticisms, Bloom's Taxonomy retains practical value when properly understood. As the Harvard Derek Bok Center for Teaching and Learning notes:

> Every discipline has some quibble with the specifics of these taxonomies. Our point is not to suggest that they are sacrosanct. Rather, we think that they are valuable as a heuristic—or even just as a lexicon of verbs for assignments—that can help you both when you are designing, and then when you are reflecting back on, your lessons and assignments and the responses of your students to them.

This characterisation—the taxonomy as heuristic rather than validated model—captures its appropriate use.

### 8.2 Common Vocabulary

The taxonomy provides a common vocabulary for discussing educational objectives. Even if that vocabulary is imprecise and inconsistently applied, having shared terminology facilitates communication among educators about learning goals and assessment design.

### 8.3 Variety Prompt

At minimum, the taxonomy serves as a reminder that there is more to learning than recall. Reviewing assessment items against its categories can prompt instructors to include more varied cognitive demands, even if the specific categorisations are debatable.

---

## 9. Conclusion

Anderson and Krathwohl's revision of Bloom's Taxonomy addressed some problems with the original while creating others and leaving many unresolved. The empirical evidence does not support treating the taxonomy as a validated model of cognitive processes: the hierarchy lacks robust support; action verbs do not reliably indicate cognitive levels; the two dimensions are related rather than independent; the discrete categories artificially fragment interconnected processes; the higher-order/lower-order distinction obscures within-category complexity; and the framework predates and remains disconnected from contemporary cognitive science.

None of this implies that the taxonomy is worthless. As a rough heuristic for generating variety in instructional design and assessment, it retains value. What cannot be justified is the dogmatic adherence common in educational practice—presenting the taxonomy without criticism, treating its categories as natural kinds, and using its verb lists as reliable guides to cognitive processes.

Teacher education programmes would serve their students better by presenting Bloom's Taxonomy in its proper context: as one among several imperfect frameworks for thinking about learning, useful for prompting reflection but not for precision measurement. The critical assessment conspicuously absent from most educational training is precisely what this influential framework most requires.
